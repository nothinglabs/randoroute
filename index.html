Routing settings

Limits

Minimum shoulder is the width required on faster roads. Max speed without shoulder lets slower roads pass even where shoulder data is missing. Never allow roads faster than makes roads above the selected speed fail; “no cutoff” leaves the shoulder rule in charge.

Presets

Each preset replaces your active routing rules and route preferences. Use the Rules button on a preset card to see its complete settings. Changing any individual control afterward switches you to custom rules.

Options

  • Heavily prefer bike routes & trails — every route choice favors bike facilities and designated corridors; physical infrastructure gets the stronger benefit.
  • Prefer residential streets — every route choice favors streets mapped as residential or living streets.
  • Trust designated bike routes — a USBR or regional designation satisfies the shoulder rule. Turn this off to judge those roads by the same speed and shoulder limits as every other road.
  • Allow freeway as last resort — a freeway is permitted only as a heavily penalized fallback, and it still fails the rules.
  • Allow mountain bike trails — explicitly MTB-tagged paths become available but stay strongly avoided; any use is called out in Route Details. Off by default.
  • Only show routes fully matching safety rules — every option must pass all your rules, except the last ~1,000 ft needed to reach your start, waypoints, or destination; those short access blocks still show red. A very short failing run bounded by passing roads may be classified as an intersection crossing rather than riding along that road. Verify the crossing control on the map; the graph does not yet confirm a signal or crosswalk.
  • Unknown shoulder = 0 ft — faster roads must prove an adequate shoulder to pass.

Place search always keeps offline local results available; the ⌕ button also runs an online lookup.

Turn by Turn

Speak compass directions adds the heading (north, southeast, …) to each spoken turn. Status update speaks on your chosen cadence (Never up to every 30 minutes) with any combination of route status, current speed, miles remaining, and estimated time left.

Advanced routing weights

Most riders should not change these. Presets and Limits are the intended controls. Weights expose internal soft costs for testing and fine-tuning; poor combinations can produce long or unintuitive routes. They never override bicycle prohibitions or your hard Limits, and can be restored with Reset all defaults.

    Help

    Map data & routing graph

    The app combines official Washington data with OpenStreetMap and elevation into one offline, on-device routing graph. Layer switches affect only what is visible; routing still uses all available data.

    Official WSDOT data

    Bicycle Level of Traffic Stress (BLTS) supplies statewide road stress, measured shoulder context, and access-control information. Roadway Characteristic Data supplies legal speeds on state highways. Active Transportation Facilities identifies shared lanes, bike lanes, buffered or separated lanes, and shared-use paths. Permanent bicycle restrictions are hard exclusions: the router never traverses them.

    OpenStreetMap data

    OSM supplies the connected rideable road and bicycle-path network, street names, signed speeds where tagged, road class, one-way rules, shoulders, bicycle access, cycleways, designated national/regional bicycle routes, ferries, and residential streets. Missing speed limits use a documented road-class estimate; the street popup says when speed is estimated. Ways tagged bicycle=no or bicycle=dismount are not part of the riding graph. Paths explicitly tagged mtb:*, or in an OSM route=mtb relation, are hidden and excluded by default; Settings can make them available with a strong penalty.

    Elevation & curve cautions

    AWS Terrarium elevation tiles provide climb/descent, average uphill and maximum grade, and grade-aware travel time. Estimated time slows for climbing and rolling terrain; route searches also apply a modest extra preference for flatter alternatives, strongest in friendly searches. The builder looks for a tight curve that overlaps an uphill grade on a faster road without a protective facility, then adjusts concern using known shoulder width. This is a possible limited-visibility uphill curve, directional and separately reported. It is a geometry/elevation proxy—not measured sight distance—and does not by itself fail your riding rules.

    How routes are chosen

    The router tests several time/safety and road-preference combinations, removes nearly identical paths, and presents up to five meaningfully different results. Rule failures, freeways, limited access, curve cautions, speed, grade, facilities, designated routes, and residential preference affect cost. A physical bike facility receives a stronger routing benefit than designation alone; if an edge has both, the facility benefit wins. OSM tertiary, secondary, primary, and trunk classes also add increasing soft costs when no bike facility is recorded; this is a traffic-volume proxy, not a rule failure. Freeways remain a last resort; legal limited-access highways are cautions; permanent prohibitions are impassable.

    What each map layer means

    • Designated routes (USBR & regional): official national/regional OSM bicycle-route relationships, drawn as a dashed blue ribbon beneath the safety verdict. Designation is useful routing context, not proof of a bike facility. A lime facility, amber caution, or red failure remains visible on top. Turning the layer off hides only the ribbon; the router still uses the route data.
    • WSDOT BLTS (state highways): official state-road stress and shoulder/access context.
    • OSM bike infrastructure: mapped bike lanes, cycle tracks, shared-use paths, and other bicycle facilities. Off-street bicycle paths and trails use a lime line with a fine dotted center; on-street accommodations are solid. Explicit mountain-bike paths are hidden unless enabled in Settings.
    • Bikes prohibited (WSDOT): permanent state restriction data for inspection; these graph segments are excluded from routing.
    • All roads (OSM, est. speeds): the broader street network, including residential roads; its visibility is reduced when zoomed out to keep the map readable.

    Lime means a physical bike facility passes your rules; a dotted-center lime line is an off-street/shared-use trail. Dashed blue marks a designated bike route, solid blue means another road passes, amber means caution, dark red dashes mean failure, and gray means insufficient data. The planned route uses the same symbols; failing portions pulse. Use the map legend for the exact swatches.

    Limits of the data

    Coverage and tagging vary by place. A missing shoulder, bike facility, traffic volume, surface, crossing control, or sight-distance measurement is not proof that it is absent. Short intersection crossings are inferred from route geometry; they do not currently confirm a signal or crosswalk. Always use local signs and conditions; the app is a planning aid, not a guarantee of safety.
    Route help

    Choose and compare routes

    This guide picks up after Start and Destination are set and explains how to judge the route choices.

    What the route letters mean

    The app tries several safety, speed, and road-preference searches, removes near-duplicates, and shows up to five meaningfully different choices as A–E. The letters are not safety grades. Route A is the recommended practical safety balance; the others preserve useful tradeoffs or different corridors.

    Compare the summaries

    Start with distance, time, and climbing, then compare Ride mix. It reports the share on a physical trail or lane and the shares that pass, need caution, or fail your rules. Trail/lane distance overlaps the verdict percentages—it is not an additional category. The swatches match the planned route and map legend.

    Use Details before choosing

    Concerns collects rule failures, freeways, limited-access roads, mountain-bike trails, and possible uphill-curve cautions. All Steps shows the road-by-road route. The elevation profile appears with the route summary; tap it to expand. Tap a concern or step to highlight it on the map.

    Read the safety labels

    • Lime — Bike network: a recorded bike facility that passes your rules. Off-street paths and shared-use trails are dotted.
    • Dashed blue — Designated route: a signed or mapped bicycle corridor that passes your rules. Designation is context, not a physical bike facility.
    • Blue — Passes rules: another road that passes your current shoulder and speed limits.
    • Amber — Caution: a bike-legal limited-access highway. It may pass your numeric rules but deserves extra judgment.
    • Red dashed — Fails rules / unavailable: a known rule conflict, freeway, or permanent bicycle restriction. Failing planned segments pulse; permanent restrictions are never routed.
    • Gray — Insufficient data: available road data cannot support a verdict under the current settings.
    • Possible limited-visibility uphill curve: a separately reported geometry/elevation estimate, not measured sight distance. Tap it in Details to highlight it.

    How your rules affect the choices

    Changing Settings recalculates the choices. The router strongly avoids roads that fail your rules and tries to surface a fully matching route when one is practical; Only show routes fully matching safety rules removes the remaining non-matching choices. Some alternatives are discovered with a stricter no-shoulder speed search, but their safety results and map colors always use your actual settings.

    Make the final call

    A route can match the available data and still be uncomfortable in current traffic, weather, construction, or surface conditions. Use Route A as a starting point, compare genuinely different corridors, and choose the option that fits the ride you are prepared for.
